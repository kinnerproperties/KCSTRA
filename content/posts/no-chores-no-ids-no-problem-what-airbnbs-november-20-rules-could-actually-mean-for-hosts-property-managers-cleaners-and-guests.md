  actually mean for hosts, property managers, cleaners and guests

author: Stacy Johnson
description: Airbnb’s November 20 rules killed the checkout chore list and most
  guest ID checks. We asked hosts, property managers and cleaners how they
  really feel, and the answers range from furious to “meh.”
---
If your Airbnb checkout list currently reads like a chore chart from a very strict summer camp, enjoy it while it lasts. For bookings made on or after **November 20, 2026**, Airbnb is asking hosts to put the label maker down.

The headlines are all about dishes and bedsheets. The bigger story is quieter: for most stays, hosts can no longer ask guests for a copy of their ID, their email address, or a signature on a rental agreement. We talked to people across the Kansas City short-term rental world to see how they really feel. The answers range from "this is the end" to "meh."

One note before anyone panics: the cutoff is the **booking date**, not the stay date. A reservation made November 19 for a trip next spring still follows the old rules.

## **The checkout chore list is (mostly) dead**

Under [++Airbnb's new policy++](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/4168), hosts can't ask guests to do work a cleaner would typically do. Here's where the line falls:


|  |  |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| **No longer allowed** | **Still fair game** |
| Washing and putting away dishes | Leaving dirty dishes in the sink |
| Stripping beds or starting laundry | Gathering used towels |
| Hauling trash off-site | Sorting trash and recycling into indoor bins |
| Telling guests not to use the A/C you advertised | Asking them to turn it off when they leave |


There's room for common sense. Bear country can still require the bear-proof bin. Putting the hot tub cover back on? Fine. Testing the chlorine? That's a job, not a request.

A few other changes ride along. Hosts now pick from seven standard house rules (guest count, quiet hours, events, pets, smoking, commercial photography, locking up), and everything else goes under "Additional rules." (Airbnb first called them "requests," then renamed them after hosts said that was confusing.) All of it has to be in the listing before booking, so the surprise laminated sign on the fridge is officially retired. Hosts also can't fine guests anymore, though you can still warn them about fines your city or HOA might charge. And if a guest breaks a house rule, or says up front that they plan to, you can cancel without penalty.

Airbnb has since admitted it "could have done a better job explaining" all this, which may be the most relatable thing a tech company has said all year. Here's how enforcement works: reviews start November 20 and run through March 2027. If one of your additional rules doesn't pass, you'll get an email and at least 20 days to fix it or appeal. Appeals can include proof, like an HOA rule or a local ordinance. Keep a rule Airbnb didn't approve, and guests may see a notice on your listing that it "includes cleaning tasks."

Here's the part that blows our minds: Airbnb built a tool that reads every listing and flags a request to load the dishwasher, but it won't verify a short-term rental license number. Kansas City requires a city registration number on every listing, and Airbnb asks hosts to enter one. Whether that number is real is apparently someone else's problem. A rogue chore list gets caught in seconds. A rogue rental? Not so much.

## **The bigger deal: no more ID, no more lease**

For most bookings, hosts can no longer ask guests for:

- Contact info like phone, email or home address
- ID documents or photos
- Financial info, background checks or credit history
- Biometric data, like facial recognition

That means goodbye to the pre-arrival ID selfie, check-in apps that demand a license photo, and Wi-Fi login pages that quietly collect emails. Rental agreements survive only in a handful of cases: when the law, your insurance or an HOA requires one, for an optional amenity like a golf cart, for stays of 28+ nights, and for Airbnb Luxe.

Here's the part worth knowing: if an allowed guest agreement requires ID verification and guest contact info, then collecting them is allowed too. Matt Cool, Territory Manager of Supply at Airbnb, confirmed that on a call with us. So if your insurance requires an agreement, that agreement can do some of the heavy lifting.

You can still check a guest's ID in person at check-in, as long as your listing says so. You just can't keep a copy. Great news if you love standing in a driveway at 4 p.m. on a Saturday.

## **Who's mad, who's thrilled, and who's shrugging**

### **Guests: thrilled**

Paying a cleaning fee and then doing the cleaning has been one of the most common complaints about Airbnb for years. Guests are celebrating, and the internet has been happily roasting any host who objects.

One guest summed it up online: when checking out, some Airbnbs "feels like a military operation." Another pointed out she's used Airbnb all over Europe and has never once been asked to strip a bed.

### **Hosts: some are furious**

++[More than 10,000 hosts have signed a petition](https://fortune.com/2026/10/28/airbnb-guests-no-dishes-strip-beds-hosts-boycott-cleaning-fees/)++ against the change, and some are talking about pulling their calendars. The real sting for many isn't the dishes. It's losing ID checks and guest contact info, especially for self-check-in hosts who never meet their guests.

Read the comments under any news story about this and a theme emerges. As one commenter put it, like a spouse sitting across from a divorce lawyer: "it was never about the dishes." Many hosts say the chore list is a distraction from the real issue, which is handing the keys to someone they can't identify. (Others have more practical concerns. One wondered who's going to mow the grass now.)

There's a quieter reason, too: email capture. Plenty of hosts collect guest emails so they can invite them back directly and skip Airbnb's fees next time. Not everyone is sympathetic. Some local industry leaders see it as the risk you take building a direct booking business on someone else's platform. Honestly, if you were Airbnb, you'd probably shut it down too.

**A quick myth check**, because the comment sections are on fire: hosts can still ban smoking and vaping, since that's one of the seven standard rules. The trash rule bans asking guests to haul garbage off-site; asking them to sort it into designated bins is still fine. If you read it somewhere in all caps, check it against [++Airbnb's own policy page++](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/4168).

### **Property managers: depends who you ask**

Rob Scarborough, a KC local property manager, is decidedly calm. PMS-connected listings like his aren't on the new rules yet, though he fully expects them to be eventually. He's watched this play out before, like when Airbnb changed commissions for PMS users first. His take on chores: 97% of guests are fantastic, and the other 3% were going to be a problem either way.

"I'm not going to change my business model for the small percent of people who don't know how to act."

Not everyone thinks PMs are getting off easy. On calls with large property managers across the country, the frustration is loud. Tyann Marcink Hammond, President of the Missouri Vacation Home Alliance (MOVHA), has learned that Airbnb is most likely giving software-connected Pro Hosts more time to comply, since their software providers need technology updates first. There's no official written guidance on that yet.

"My biggest concern is a marketing platform telling a homeowner they cannot use basic tools for safety, fraud prevention and local compliance."

She went on: "I understand that Airbnb pushed these changes to make it easier for guests to book a stay, but in so doing, they've created unnecessary friction and a possible safety issue by deeming that a host may no longer ask a guest for contact information, independently verify the guest ID, or require a guest agreement to be signed unless Airbnb grants an exception. Yet Airbnb does not carry the risk and liability that the homeowner carries and is responsible for."

"Some of this may get rolled back, as past changes have been. Airbnb could also negotiate with larger managers, easing some requirements in exchange for more inventory. Translation: it's fluid. Very fluid.

Hosts are getting mixed messages, and people are squeamish when basking in the unknown.

### **Safety experts: raising a hand**

Justin Ford, a Short-Term Rental Safety Expert at Breezeway, is asking a question Airbnb hasn't answered: where does a safety instruction end and a banned house rule begin? Does a "No diving" or "Children must be supervised in the pool" sign count as a rule added after booking? He calls it a grey area. And losing guest contact info isn't just a marketing problem. In an emergency like a natural disaster, being able to reach a guest can matter.

### **Newer hosts: Wait, this is new?!**

Here's a twist we didn't expect. Some newer hosts are already living under the "new" rules. Justin Walker, owner of The Arable on Charlotte, a 4-bedroom, 2-bath home in Downtown KC, started hosting in April 2026. His account already blocks sharing contact info in the chat and limits house rules the way the new policy describes. So this isn't really new for everyone. It's new for the hosts who have been around long enough to remember doing it the old way, and they're the ones who have to change.

And the sky hasn't fallen. TurnTidy KC cleans The Arable, and nobody has trashed the place. Guests typically leave it in great condition, no chore list required. Granted, those guests probably had no idea the rules were any different. Which kind of proves the point: most people tidy up because they're decent, not because a list told them to. The real test comes once the headlines tell every guest they're off the hook.

### **Cleaners: you'd think we'd be panicking**

I own TurnTidy KC, and we specialize in STR cleaning, so I do have to consider this from an operational standpoint. On paper, cleaners take the hit. The chores don't vanish; they move to the turnover crew. Dishes, beds, and trash can add the better part of an hour to a turn, and most turns already happen in a tight window between a 10 or 11 a.m. checkout and a 3 or 4 p.m. check-in.

The crews who will be feeling it the most are the ones that have counted on guests to start the first load of laundry, especially in big houses with a lot of beds. That head start is gone, and the check-in time may not move to make up for it. Those companies will have to pivot, whether that means more hands, more linen sets, or a different laundry setup, just to hit the same turnover times.

Many cleaners are saying the same thing. Many who provide same-day turns in a 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. window are paid hourly and will simply have to charge more. A detail most people miss: a stripped bed tells the crew which beds were actually slept in. And hosts who used that head start to offer early check-in say that perk may be the first casualty.

Which raises a question for hosts: is a 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. turnover window still realistic? Pushing it to 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. buys an extra hour or two for the crew, and most guests won't notice. It beats a guest pulling into the driveway while the sheets are still in the dryer.

I'm not losing sleep. As the owner, I've built systems that support our cleaners in every situation, so tight turnovers get done efficiently, with or without the guests' help. We already handle most laundry offsite, so stripped beds barely change our routine. We have always worked to accommodate each host's rules, whatever they are. And honestly? Plenty of guests never did the checkout list anyway. What we care about isn't whether someone loaded the dishwasher. It's whether they left the home considerately.

## **So, will cleaning fees go up?**

Probably, at least for some listings. Airbnb doesn't cap cleaning fees, and hosts can't charge guests for skipping chores, so the extra labor has to be paid for somewhere. Many hosts expect it to land on cleaning companies first and on guests' totals after that. The chores didn't disappear. They just got a price tag.

There's a multiplier, too. Airbnb's 15.5% host fee applies to the cleaning fee, not just the nightly rate, so every dollar a cleaner adds grows a little before it reaches the guest's total. Guests may not be getting the deal they think they are.

## **Before November 20**

**Hosts:** Rewrite your rules in Airbnb's new editor, delete any fines, scrub your automated messages of ID and contract requests, and check that your check-in and Wi-Fi tools don't collect guest info. Then ask your cleaner whether your check-in time still works.

**And keep watching.** Some in the industry expect parts of this to get rolled back, and the details are still shifting. Pay attention to Airbnb's updates, stay educated, and lean on your local associations. Heck, write a letter to Airbnb corporate. Then adjust as needed, if you choose to stay on the platform.

**Guests:** Read the house rules section before you book. That's the whole list now. No surprises allowed.

## **The bottom line**

Policy changes come and go. As Rob put it, "we're all still here. The money is still here." The rulebook got shorter, but the one rule that has always mattered hasn't changed: be a good human and take care of each other. Leave the place the way you'd want to find it, and you'll never have to worry about a chore list again.
