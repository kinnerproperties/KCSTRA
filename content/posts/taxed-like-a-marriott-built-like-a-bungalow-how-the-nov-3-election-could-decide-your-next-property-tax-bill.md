If you run a short-term rental anywhere in the Kansas City area, you've had a busy couple of years.

Not with guests. With government.

Here's the short version. Kansas City banned new non-owner-occupied rentals from residential neighborhoods in 2023. Jackson County tried to tax our homes like hotels in 2025. And the state fix that passed both chambers in 2026 died in the final hours of session. Three governments, one industry, zero permanent solutions.

Below: what we're up against, the myths driving it, and the races on your Nov. 3 ballot that will decide what happens next.

## **Where things stand**

### **The City: no new rentals in your own neighborhood**

In May 2023, the Kansas City Council unanimously banned new non-owner-occupied short-term rentals in residentially zoned areas. Rentals already registered were grandfathered. Owner-occupied rentals aren't limited by zoning. The push came from neighborhood groups citing trash, noise and crime.

Translation: if you don't live on-site, new rentals can only open in commercial zones. That's a tough sell, since most guests want to stay in actual neighborhoods, close to the events, family and friends they're in town to see.

### **The County: your house is now a hotel (on paper)**

In 2025, the Jackson County Assessor reclassified short-term rentals from residential to commercial. That means a home gets assessed at 32% of its market value instead of 19%. It also removes the 15% annual cap on residential increases. One host's two-bed, one-bath went from $2,100 in property taxes to $6,800.

County Legislator Sean Smith introduced Ordinance 5987, which passed on June 18, 2025. It pauses reclassification until the county builds a countywide registration and reporting system. Read that again: a pause, not a fix. The next assessment cycle is 2027.

### **The State: so close, and yet**

Rep. Chris Brown's bill to keep single-family rentals classified as residential passed the House 118-34 in 2025. It never got a Senate vote.

In 2026, the Senate passed its version 30-3, and the House passed one too. Then the House folded it into a bigger property tax package, adding a 15-home cap on corporate owners. When session ended May 15 without a property tax deal, the fix went down with the ship. Who testified against it? The hotel and lodging lobby. Shocking, we know.

The bottom line: Missouri law hasn't changed. Nothing stops an assessor from trying this again.

## **Myths vs. facts**

Most of the numbers below come from a 2026 regional study commissioned by the Kansas City Regional Association of REALTORS and conducted by RRC Associates, covering six counties and 2025 data.

### **Myth: Short-term rentals are run by Wall Street**

**Fact:** 95% of operators in the KC region manage a single unit, and nearly 90% of properties are locally owned.

The "corporate rental empire" is mostly your neighbor with one house and a very organized cleaning schedule.

### **Myth: Short-term rentals are swallowing our housing**

**Fact:** They're 0.4% of the region's housing, about one home in every 243. The study found no statistically significant link to rising home values or rents.

Housing affordability is a real problem in Kansas City. One home in 243 isn't what's causing it.

### **Myth: It's basically a hotel, so tax it like one**

**Fact:** These are single-family homes. They're zoned residential, financed as homes, insured as homes and sold on the residential market.

Critics argue that a property earning income and collecting sales tax is a business. But long-term rentals earn income too, and nobody's taxing those like a Marriott. As Sean Smith put it: "what about people who are working from home?"

Last we checked, hotels don't come with a backyard, a mailbox and a neighbor named Barb.

### **Myth: Most short-term rentals operate illegally anyway**

**Fact:** A city audit found about 93% of units operated without a permit between 2018 and 2022. That was a real problem, and the industry said so. Licensed hosts publicly welcomed holding unlicensed operators accountable.

The answer is enforcement and registration. Punishing licensed hosts for unlicensed ones is like ticketing every car in the lot because one guy parked on the sidewalk.

### **Myth: Neighbors want them gone**

**Fact:** The study's public opinion survey found residents see the economic and tourism benefits and also have neighborhood concerns. Overall, they favored clear rules and accountability over strict limits or outright bans.

Noise and trash complaints are legitimate. Enforce the rules on bad actors and leave the good neighbors alone.

### **Myth: Taxing rentals like hotels means more money for the county**

**Fact:** The Missouri Vacation Home Alliance points to a St. Louis home that generated about $19,000 a year in sales and lodging taxes. When its property tax jumped from $8,000 to $20,000, it shut down. Local governments lost more than they gained.

Regionally, short-term rentals produced more than $34 million in state and local taxes in 2025, plus $624 million in economic output and about 4,000 jobs. You can't collect lodging tax from a house nobody's lodging in.

### **Myth: The tax fight is over**

**Fact:** Jackson County's pause has no end date, only a condition. The state fix died. The 2027 assessment cycle is coming, and Jackson County elects a new County Executive on Nov. 3. Both candidates say assessment reform is a priority.

It's not over. It's halftime.

## **The races that matter**

All three levels of this fight have seats on the Nov. 3 ballot. These four races matter most for hosts in the KC area.

### **Missouri House District 16 (Clay County): Rep. Chris Brown (R)**

Brown is the House sponsor of the bill to keep short-term rentals taxed as residential. He's a REALTOR and a retired North Kansas City teacher. He faces Democrat Dan Kinate, a retired electrician running on affordability. Brown won about 56% here in 2024, so this Northland seat is far from safe.

If our sponsor loses, the bill needs a new champion in January.

### **Missouri Senate District 8 (Lee's Summit, Blue Springs): Speaker Jon Patterson (R) vs. Rep. Keri Ingle (D)**

Patterson currently runs the Missouri House. Now he's running for an open Senate seat in one of the few competitive Senate races in the state. A Republican-sponsored poll from early October had Ingle ahead 48% to 45%.

Patterson's campaign leads with property tax relief, which is exactly the conversation we want to be in.

### **Missouri House District 34 (Lee's Summit): Sean Smith (R) vs. Rep. Kemp Strickler (D)**

Rep. Kemp Strickler is the incumbent, first elected in 2022 by 37 votes. In his first term, he was the ranking member on the House tax reform committee and sat on the property tax reform committee. Sean Smith is a Jackson County Legislator who introduced the 2025 ordinance pausing reclassification. Both have worked on property taxes, and this is a close district.

Whoever wins will vote on our bill, so meet both. Smith's county work dealt with our issue directly, which saves a lot of explaining.

### **Jackson County Executive: Stacy Lake (D) vs. Alan Rohlfing (R)**

Both candidates name assessment reform as a priority. Lake has said she's very concerned about the 2027 property tax cycle, and she's argued the County Executive has significant control over the assessment department. Democrats have carried this office easily in recent elections, so Lake is the favorite.

Our goal: make sure whoever wins hears from hosts before the 2027 assessment cycle starts, not after the notices hit mailboxes.

### **The best time to meet them is now**

Candidates are never more accessible than when they're asking for your vote. Fundraisers, meet-and-greets and neighborhood coffees are where real conversations happen. After January, you're one of thousands of emails.

- **Ask for the schedule.** Call or email the campaign and ask when its next event is. Every campaign lists contact info on its website.
- **Bring one story and one number.** Your story, and what reclassification would do to your tax bill.
- **Meet both candidates in a close race.** Whoever wins votes on our bill.
- **Bring the data.** Some candidates get it in one sentence. Others want the numbers: 95% single-unit owners, 0.4% of housing, $34 million in state and local taxes.
- **Remember who's nervous.** Close races mean candidates are listening. Use that.

## **What you can do this week**

1. **Show up Thursday.** The KCSTRA meetup is Thursday, Oct. 15 at 6:00 PM at 117 W Armour Blvd, with a Kansas City regulations update and Q&A. Bring a friend who hosts.
2. **Know your number.** Run your property through the [++MOVHA property tax calculator++](https://movha.org/tax-calculator/) to see what commercial reclassification would do to your bill. One real number beats a ten-minute speech.
3. **Find your people.** Look up your State Representative and State Senator with the [++Missouri legislator lookup++](https://www.senate.mo.gov/legislookup/default), and know your County Legislator too. You can't make your case to someone if you don't know who they are.
4. **Follow them online.** It's 2026. Your candidates are on Facebook, Instagram and X, and that's where they post their events, town halls and fundraisers. Follow, comment respectfully, and share your hosting story where they'll see it. A thoughtful comment from a local constituent gets noticed more than you'd think.
5. **Vote.** No-excuse early voting runs Oct. 20 through Nov. 2. Mail-in absentee requests are due Oct. 21. Election Day is Nov. 3. Hosts who don't vote are just guests with opinions.
6. **Tell your story.** MOVHA is collecting owner stories: how many people you hire, how many guests you've hosted, and how much sales and lodging tax you've remitted. [++Share yours here++](https://movha.org/bills/).

**Bonus round, Wednesday night:** Eastern Jackson County chambers of commerce are hosting a forum for Jackson County Legislature candidates, the same body that passed the tax pause. [++Register through the Lee's Summit Chamber++](https://leessummitchamberofcommerce.growthzoneapp.com/calendar) and confirm the start time.

## **The bottom line**

Three governments made these calls, and the people who make the next ones are being chosen right now. The 2027 session and the 2027 assessment cycle are coming either way. The only question is whether hosts are in the room when the decisions get made.

So go meet them while they still need your vote.
